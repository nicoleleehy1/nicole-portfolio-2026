1. Background information

1.1 Introduction to Burnout

Burnout Stress Syndrome is a common phenomenon resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been managed adequately (“Long Working Hours…”). First introducing this syndrome in 1974, Herbert Freudenberger defined Burnout as a “state of mental and physical exhaustion caused by one’s personal life.” Since then, Burnout has been one of the most widely discussed mental health problems in modern society, with the number of papers published exponentially grow over the past fifty years (Figure 1) (De Hert).

Maslach described Burnout as “a fire that cannot continue burning unless there are sufficient resources that keep being replenished”. Although Burnout has not been formally recognised as a mental disorder, the World Health Organisation recognises the prevalence of this global issue as an occupational phenomenon under the 11th Revision of the International Classification of Diseases. It states that Burnout can be characterised by three indicators - symptoms of “energy depletion or exhaustion”, “increased mental distance from one’s job, or feelings of negativism or cynicism” and “reduced efficacy”. Employees who experience this syndrome feel emotionally exhausted and lose a sense of personal accomplishment, causing them to feel unable to provide impactful contributions to their work.

1.2 Global Significance of Burnout

A 2019 UN report stated that “stress, excessively-long working hours and disease contribute to nearly 2.8 million deaths of workers” annually. Amid the COVID-19 pandemic, Burnout reached a record high of 43% in over 100 countries, rising from 39% in 2019 (“Stress, Overtime, Disease…”).

In addition to extreme cases of death, Burnout has posed major health threats to workers. Findings indicated that people who work 55+ hours have an estimated 35% higher risk of stroke and 17% higher risk of dying from heart disease (“Long working hours…”). Among the 10,000 employees investigated by Asana in 2022, 70% reported Burnout symptoms, including 62% reportedly developing lower morale (emotional exhaustion) while 43% of workers grew less engaged with time (cynicism).

Not only does the well-being of workers negatively affect individual productivity in workplaces, companies also find themselves having to pay the price. Employees with burnout are “63% more likely to take a sick day” (Seppälä). In addition to lower revenue output, Burnout placed high medical costs on businesses. According to the American Institute of Stress, Burnout costs the US economy $300 billion annually (Boyd).

1.3 Local Manifestation - Reason for choosing Scandinavian Physicians

Burnout is an individual stress experience often involving the person’s perception of both themselves and others. According to the ILO Encyclopaedia, Burnout is prevalent among “service occupations where: a) the relationship between providers and recipients is central to the job; and b) the provision of service, care, treatment or education can be a highly emotional experience” (“Burnout”).

The incidence of Burnout is most evident among physicians, who fulfil the above criteria. Physicians’ nature of work is highly demanding - it requires them to be at the front lines of medical care. A systematic review encompassing 30 observational studies had pooled an overall 52% prevalence of Burnout among doctors (Ghahramani). One reason for this high prevalence is physicians are responsible for patients’ lives and have the pressure of not committing any irreversible errors which could compromise patients’ care. According to Medscape (“Medscape Physician Burnout and Depression Report 2023”), the rate of burnout has risen to 53% in 2022, from 43% in 2020, and increased 26% since 2018. Therefore, this essay will focus on Physicians as determining the cause for this high susceptibility of Burnout gives greater confidence for the applicability of the findings.

In particular, physicians in Scandinavian countries were chosen as the focus for this essay. The Scandinavian region consists of three countries: Denmark, Norway, and Sweden. All three Scandinavian countries consistently thrive in the top 10 ranks every year (Helliwell et al., 19), with healthier life expectancy, stronger social support and higher GDP per capita. Work-life balance has been a consistent priority for the government - their Burnout rate is ranked lowest among European countries (Figure 2). Despite Scandinavian countries being known for their low stress, however, there are varying levels of Burnout in physicians across these countries. Unlike the other professions in the population, Norwegian and Danish physicians carry high levels of Burnout. However, Swedish physicians continue to sustain low levels of Burnout. (Figure 3)

This essay will draw conclusions of whether the environmental factors increasing the susceptibility of predisposed genes are the major cause for Burnout. This will be done by: 1) comparing the stressful work environment of physicians to the non-stressful environment among the rest of the population using the Job Demand-Control model and Effort-Reward Imbalance model and 2) investigating why Norwegian and Danish physicians have a higher rate of Burnout compared to Sweden physicians.

1.4 Interdisciplinary Approach

When discussing the etiology of Burnout, the concepts of Biology and Psychology are often intertwined. Specifically, the stressors in a physician’s environment play a crucial role in the emergence of Burnout symptoms through interacting with certain genes (Serotonin Transporter Gene (SLC6A4) and Glucocorticoid Receptor Gene (NR3C1)), causing them to be expressed. As a result, a person is more susceptible to imbalances in their neurotransmitter (serotonin) and hormonal (cortisol) levels respectively, causing the person to become less motivated, stressed and exhausted. This essay will explore how the environment “triggers” predisposed genetics to become expressed through the Diathesis Stress model (Caspi et al.) and epigenetics explanation.

1.5 Evaluation of Sources and Methodology

The disciplines of Biology and Psychology will be explored through a variety of credible sources. Research for both the Biological and Psychological explanation consists of using Scientific journals and articles. These publications provide highly accurate information due to the rigorous peer reviews by experts in the field. To apply these theories in Scandinavian countries, research from focal case studies and statistics were used. These methods provide rich qualitative and quantitative data on the behaviour of Scandinavian physicians and employees, allowing comparisons between those in stressful and non-stressful environments to be made. This makes findings supporting the epigenetic explanation more generalisable to the target group: Scandinavian physicians.

2. Psychological Explanation - Environment

This discipline examines the psychological aspect of work-related stress experienced by Scandinavian doctors. Psychology uses theories to study human behaviour and thoughts. Environmental stressors, such as the demanding nature of one’s work, organisational culture and societal expectations, can expose workers to a high-stress work environment, causing them to be more susceptible to Burnout.

2.1 Stages of Burnout Stress Syndrome

Freudenberger first developed a 12-stage model explaining the onset of Burnout Stress Syndrome following his qualitative observations of the staff at a free clinic for drug addicts (Figure 4). The excessive demands resulted in the exhaustion, impulsivity and depression of workers (Freudenberger, 2).

He then simplified the model to 5 stages, in which physical, mental and emotional exhaustion increases as a person progresses. From a physician’s perspective, one initially goes through the “honeymoon” phase, in which they feel enthusiastic about their job, putting their medical knowledge and skills to help those in need. However, over time, this initial excitement gives way to the experience of job-related stress, including the pressure of providing the best quality of care to patients. Many physicians' lives become limited to work, causing them to neglect their personal priorities and common stress symptoms to emerge. (De Stefan)

This eventually leads to the stage of chronic stress, encompassing feelings of frustration, failure and powerlessness. Efforts do not visibly pay off and physicians’ lack of acknowledgement of these symptoms contributes to feelings of incompetence (De Stefan). This results in the stage of apathy, characterised by pessimistic outlooks and self-doubt, subsequently causing physicians to lack motivation. Finally, habitual burnout occurs - chronic sadness, fatigue and depression grow apparent. These three symptoms become the indicators for determining Burnout, as stated in ICD-11’s definition.

2.2 Environmental Stressors inducing Burnout Stress Syndrome

2.2.1 Environmental Stressors Involved in patient care

Burnout is most susceptible to human service workers as they have the unique pressure of “utilising one’s self as the “tool” in face-to-face work”. (Kumar) Physicians fall into this category of high risk as their jobs centre around patient care. Some sources of stress include physicians’ need to rescue patients, a sense of frustration or powerlessness when a patient’s illness progresses, loss or grief, and uncertainty in clinical practice.

Karasek’s Job Demand-Control Model focuses on the relationship between job demands vs level of job control and an employee has (ie. design latitude) (Figure 6). Job demand represents the physical, emotional and psychological requirements in a workplace that may initiate an employee’s stress. On the other hand, job control refers to the level of decision-making power that an employee can have when managing their workload. Seeing as physicians have long working hours, high patient volumes and repetitive exposure to stress, they are categorised as having high-strain jobs. According to the Job Demand-Control model, when job demands are high and job control is low, doctors may feel overwhelmed, stressed and burned out.

2.2.2 Environmental stressors involved in organisational cultures

The environment in which doctors practice outside the doctor-patient relationship can be “litigious and unforgiving” (Kumar). For example, physicians may be prone to lawsuits or disputes about critical patient care, leading to stages of chronic stress and apathy according to Freudenberger’s model. Other sources, including bureaucratic environments, the requirement to always keep in touch with rapidly changing medical knowledge, and limited healthcare resources causing an unforgiving environment for mistakes (Kumar), make physicians feel as though their efforts do not visibly pay off.

According to Siegrist’s (1996) Effort-Reward Imbalance model, the discrepancy between the amount of effort exerted in their work and the rewards received can lead to a higher susceptibility to Burnout. In this case, if physicians do not feel a sense of accomplishment and deal with many losses or frustrations in their work, they are likely to develop Burnout.

According to both models, when job demands are high but job control is also high, physicians may feel more empowered and are able to manage their workload more effectively. Since physicians must still place high efforts to meet the high demands of the job, one way to motivate them is through higher rewards, such as higher salaries or more time to manage personal and family responsibilities.

2.3 Application of Psychological Explanation to Scandinavian Countries

As mentioned in Section 1.3 (Local Manifestation), Norwegian and Danish physicians carry high levels of Burnout despite Scandinavian populations generally having lower stress. This can be explained by the highly demanding nature of physicians’ work, as explained by the Job Demand-Control model (Karasek) and Effort-Reward Imbalance model (Siegrist).

Rosta et al. conducted a longitudinal study investigating the changes in the level of work stress for Norweigan doctors from 2010 to 2019 using the “Effort-Reward Imbalance Questionnaire”. A representative sample of 1500-2200 doctors filled out the 5 Likert scale questionnaire. Reported results showed that General Practitioners’ effort scale (ES) significantly increase, with a score of 2.96 in 2010 to 3.51 in 2019, and that their reward scale (RS) significantly reduced, with a mean score of 4.27 in 2010 to 3.67 in 2019 (Figure 8). Another study also showed a significant decrease in job satisfaction among Norwegian physicians from 2010 to 2017, such as less freedom in their management schedules, rate of pay and working hours (Rosta).

Similar to Norway, the prevalence of Burnout among Danish physicians is higher than in the general population. A nationwide study found that Danish General practitioners with the most occupational distress had the highest association with lower job satisfaction, poor well-being and Burnout. It was also found that GPs with burnout had a significantly higher likelihood of their patients changing doctors. (Busk Nørøxe et al.) This shows that poor workplace environments not only risk physicians to more occupational distress and Burnout, but also comprimise the quality of medical care for patients.

Swedish physicians, on the other hand, sustain low Burnout rates that are similar to the rest of the population. According to the Swedish Healthy Physicians Study 2022, clinical burnout among physicians was 4.7% (Hagqvist et al.). One reason for this low prevalence is that Sweden was early to recognise Burnout as a medical diagnosis, which helped break down taboos and encouraged more to come forward to diagnose their condition (Egerstrom). This made more businesses aware and accepting of the problem, counteracting the common stigmatisation in other countries. Moreover, Sweden’s welfare system allows those diagnosed with Burnout to receive paid sick leave of up to 80% of their salary. This means people are less likely to suffer economically and there is a speedy recovery for those who suffer exhaustion.

Despite the stressful nature of physician’s jobs, Swedish doctors receive more financial incentives and a higher sense of agency in their work. Therefore, their job climbs from “High strain job” to the “Active job” category in the Job Demand-Control model, motivating them and reducing Burnout in the country.

3. Biosocial Explanation

Environmental appraisal is not enough to fully explain the susceptibility of Burnout, as not all physicians have Burnout despite being exposed to the same stressful environment. Hence, it is important to look at the interactions between predisposed vulnerability (ie. the genetic component) and the environment.

3.1 Diathesis Stress Model

The Diathesis Stress model explains the development of Burnout through environmental events interacting with hereditary predisposed genes (Simona). It argues that an individual may have several predisposed genes towards a certain behaviour (ie. the diathesis) but will only be “switched on” when they face a stressful environment, triggering its respective biological processes.

Being exposed to a stressful environment can “trigger” epigenetic mechanisms that regulate gene expression. Studies have shown that a stressful environment causes an increase in DNA methylation, causing DNA to bind more tightly to histone proteins and inhibiting the accessibility of transcription factors from forming heterochromatin. This thereby causes a person to show Burnout symptoms.

3.2 Epigenetic Mechanisms (DNA Methylation) inducing Burnout

Epigenetics is the study of how environmental factors can vary the regulation of gene expression, or the “switching on” of genes, without altering the DNA base sequence, thereby enhancing or inhibiting an individual from exhibiting a behaviour. Epigenetic studies have indicated that environmental stressors found in Scandinavian physicians’ jobs commonly trigger DNA methylation of predisposed genes, subsequently increasing the genetic susceptibility to Burnout (Bakusic et al.).

DNA methylation involves DNA methyltransferase enzymes adding a methyl (CH3) group to Cytosines in the CpG dinucleotides, which results in it being methylated to form 5-methylcytosines (Edwards). This changes the chemical property of cytosine and makes it more difficult for RNA polymerase to bind, inhibiting transcription and expression of the gene. This process occurs frequently in CpG sites of the promoter region, which are regions of DNA where “a cytosine nucleotide is followed by a guanine nucleotide in the linear sequence of bases along its 5’ → 3’ direction” (“CpG sites”). Nucleosomes may also be methylated and hence increase the DNA’s ability to bind to the histone proteins. When DNA is wrapped more tightly in nucleosomes, the accessibility to transcription factors is inhibited, hence silencing the gene into heterochromatin and causing it to become unexpressed.

3.3 Genes involved in the susceptibility of Burnout

A systematic review indicated that the Serotonin Transporter Gene (SLC6A4), which affects the reuptake of serotonin, and the Glucocorticoid Receptor Gene (NR3C1), which affects cortisol and cortisone levels, are two of the most commonly investigated and associated genes affecting susceptibility to Burnout (Bakusic et al.)

3.3.1 DNA Methylation and the Serotonin Transporter gene (SLC6A4)

Serotonin, or 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT), is a neurotransmitter responsible for mood and emotion. The serotonin transporter gene (SLC6A4), ie. the 5-HTT gene, encodes a protein called the serotonin transporter (SERT) (“Serotonin Transporter”), which reuptakes serotonin from the synaptic cleft back into the presynaptic neuron, preventing it from diffusing down the concentration gradient of the synapse and binding to the upstream neuron.

A systematic review (Bakusic) found that DNA Methylation of Burnout was most consistently found in the promoter region of the serotonin transporter gene (SLC6A4). Hypermethylation of this gene has a consistent correlation with burnout, work stress and depression in many studies. Alasaari et al. reported that work stress, after mutually adjusted for bivariate analysis, was a crucial component associated with higher levels of DNA methylation in five CpG sites of the SLC6A4 gene’s promoter as seen in a Finland nurse cohort. Moreover, a study comparing depression concordance in monozygotic twins showed that the affected twin showed a higher DNA methylation compared to the unaffected co-twin. (Zhao et al.)

The SLC6A4 gene, in particular, is “characterised by a polymorphism in the promoter region called the serotonin-transporter-linked polymorphic region (5-HTTLPR), defined by a short (S) and long (L) allele” (Bakusic). Studies have shown that those genetically predisposed to carry the S allele are more susceptible to stressful life events and depressive symptoms, aligning with the Diathesis-Stress model (Caspi et al.). For example, Duman et al. found that the rise in chronic stress and depressive symptoms correlated with increased methylation levels of the SLC6A4 gene only in homozygous carriers of the S allele. Philibert et al.’s findings supported this - there were higher methylation levels in individuals with the short (S) 5HTTLPR allele among those with Major Depressive Disorder.

Overall, stressful events induce increased methylation. The short (S) 5HTTLPR allele causes the SLC6A4 gene to be more susceptible to methylation. After methylated, this reduces the SERT protein and reduces the reuptake of serotonin. This decreases the efficiency and volume of the neurotransmitter and causes a person to show exhaustion and depressive symptoms.

3.3.2 - DNA Methylation and the Glucocorticoid Receptor gene (NR3C1)

Glucocorticoids are secreted as an endocrine response to stress. The Glucocorticoid receptor (NR3C1 or nuclear receptor subfamily 3, group C, member 1), encodes the glucocorticoid receptor (GR) protein (“Glucocorticoid Receptor”). The GR binds to the hormone cortisol, a type of glucocorticoid, which is needed to regulate the body’s response to stress. An increase in GR expression causes it to become more sensitive to cortisol, modulating the HPA axis and a more effective regulation of stress response (Wadji et al.)

With Burnout, studies have suggested that individuals may exhibit impaired GR signalling. Marchand et al. found that increased cortisol in stressed workers is correlated with stress and depression while exhaustion is followed by decreased cortisol. As depressive symptoms, stress and exhaustion are all symptoms of Burnout, this shows that the susceptibility of Burnout can be explained through varying DNA methylation patterns on the GR gene.

Menke et al’s findings showed that hypermethylation and a subsequent reduction in GR gene expression is associated with Major depressive disorder. This means that HPA-axis hyperreactivity and hypercortisolism lead to symptoms of persistent sadness or reduced efficacy, another common symptom of Burnout. In addition, Witzman et al. found that chronic stress induced an overall increase in methylation levels of the gene in the adrenal and pituitary glands, which reduced the GR expression. This reduces the GR’s ability to bind to cortisol and reduces the ability to downregulate the stress response, causing the prolonged activation of stress pathways and the emergence of Burnout symptoms.

Conversely, Menke et al. found that job-related exhaustion, a common symptom of Burnout, correlates with decreased methylation levels, which increases GR expression and hypersensitivity. This enhances GR to become more sensitive to cortisol, leading to a negative feedback inhibition on the HPA axis and hypocortisolism. When cortisol levels are too low, this explains symptoms of reduced energy levels and exhaustion in Burnout.

3.4 Significance of Genetic Component

Research from twin studies comparing Monozygotic (MZ) twins and Dizygotic (DZ) twins reveal whether genes or the environment share a greater role in causing Burnout. MZ twins share 100% of the same genes whereas DZ twins share 50%. Comparing concordance rates can indicate the genetic tendency of Burnout. (Merikangas). If the concordance rate between MZ twins is similar to that between DZ twins, environmental stressors are more important for the appraisal of Burnout. Conversely, if there is a higher concordance in MZ than in DZ twins, this shows predisposed genes precede environmental influence.

Blom et. al investigated the genetic susceptibility to Burnout in Swedish Monozygotic and Dizygotic twins. Participants responded to the adjectives “depressed”, “emotionally exhausted” and “feeling run down” using a seven-point Likert scale questionnaire. Findings from the genetic analysis revealed that Monozygotic twins are significantly more similar than Dizygotic twins, showing that predisposed genes are more dominant than environmental influence. Results showed that “genetic factors explain 33% of the individual differences in Burnout symptoms”. Middeldorp et al. supported this by showing that heritability for exhaustion in Burnout is 33% among men, which coincides with the genetic effect of 33% shown in Swedish Twins.

Both the SLC6A4 and NR3C1 genes are found in all humans. However, just saying that genetics explain the entire etiology of Burnout does not account for why only 33% of Burnout is hereditary. The other 67% would explain why, even if genes are predisposed, environmental stressors are needed to trigger Burnout, aligning with the Diathesis Stress Model and Biosocial explanation.

4. Conclusion and Discussion

In conclusion, the biosocial explanation investigating how environmental stressors trigger DNA methylation of the SLC6A4 and NR3C1 genes allows us to understand the etiology of Burnout. Higher methylation on the SLC6A4 gene reduces the expression of SERT and the reuptake of serotonin. This reduces the availability of serotonin and causes depressive symptoms. In contrast, a mix of hypermethylation and hypomethylation on the NR3C1 gene causes Burnout due to impaired GR signalling. Hypermethylation is associated with depression and chronic stress symptoms - GR expression is high, causing hypocortisolism and reduced downregulation of stress. Hypomethylation is associated with exhaustion symptoms - GR expression is low, causing hypercortisolism and prolonged activation of stress pathways.

Only isolating the psychological explanation and arguing that Burnout is only developed through the appraisal of environmental factors does not explain why only a portion of physicians develop Burnout, seeing as they are all exposed to the same stressful environment. Similarly, only isolating the genetic explanation of Burnout would be incredibly reductionist as it fails to consider the complexity of the environment around them. Hence, a strength of considering the Biosocial explanation is that it takes a holistic, comprehensive approach and investigates how various environmental stressors trigger predisposed genes. It explains why Norweigian and Danish physicians have a higher prevalence of Burnout than Swedish physicians, as their predisposed genes are exposed to a more stressful environment.

A strength of the findings in this essay is that most epigenetic studies used are held in Scandinavian countries. This means that the findings are generalisable to the Scandinavian physician population. Intense research using modern techniques such as genetic and DNA profiling has provided much evidence for the Biological explanation of developing Burnout. Many studies have discovered a strong correlation between DNA methylation and imbalances in neurotransmitter and hormonal levels, thereby causing symptoms of Burnout.

Environmental stressors became quantified using a range of research methods, such as interviews and questionnaires. Likert scale questionnaires collect a large amount of quantitative data whereas interviews collect a range of rich qualitative data. A mixture of both is necessary when understanding the multifaceted nature of Burnout, thereby increasing the internal validity of the findings in this essay.

The findings of this study can be applied to predict the likelihood of Burnout to develop among physicians. Genetic analysis of the SLC6A4 and NR3C1 genes could be a form of diagnosis to measure the true severity of Burnout. It can also be used to detect symptoms early, and adequate interventions can also be provided to minimise environmental stressors in physicians’ workplaces. This is supported by the reduction of Burnout among Swedish physicians, as policies, such as paid sick leave and medical programs that encourage individuals to recognise Burnout as an issue, reduce Burnout.

However, seeing as there is no set international diagnostic, the data for the prevalence of Burnout in Scandinavian countries varies widely. According to a systematic review, the overall prevalence of Burnout among physicians globally ranged from 0% to 80.5% as the findings were drawn from different methodologies with different cut-off points (Rotenstein). This may be because it is hard to quantify what is considered “stressful”, as everyone’s interpretations are different. A majority of the studies used in this essay use self-reported data. Respondents may perceive situations differently - for example, a person may see a situation more negatively than others because of their personality or cultural upbringing, causing them to exaggerate their experiences to conform to demand characteristics they believe they are expected to exhibit.

The Job Demand-control model, Effort-Reward imbalance model and Diathesis stress model are likely to be overly simplistic explanations. Some people, such as pro-bono workers, may enjoy putting in effort and being challenged in their jobs, despite not receiving rewards such as praise or financial gain. Other personalities, such as Type D personality, may contribute to the susceptibility of Burnout. Type D personality, defined by high levels of negative affectivity (eg. stress) and social inhibition to avoid disapproval from others, is common among 23% of medical units, and those who have it are five times more likely to have high risk of Burnout (Geuens et al.).

Seeing as studies are limited to Scandinavian countries, findings may not be generalisable to physicians in other countries. Scandinavian culture prioritises mental health and has an overall smaller prevalence of Burnout to others - there may be more complex factors in work cultures among other countries that could affect physicians’ susceptibility to Burnout. Hence, this simplified biosocial approach may not be the full explanation of Burnout. More investigation is needed to increase reliability of findings.
