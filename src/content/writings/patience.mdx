## part 1

now that i'm in my third year in the states, i've been thinking a lot about how much i rely on automation.

every morning, i ride my e-scooter to school. i've gotten so used to it that i now leave the house less than five minutes before anything starts.

when i went back home to hong kong, i noticed two things. 1) i was incredibly out of shape, and 2) i had a grudging reluctance to take public transport. everything felt slower and less optimized, even though i grew up on one of the most convenient transit systems in the world and used to know its maze of bus routes by heart. in those two weeks i was home, i got lost several times taking the bus - and giving up for a taxi. after 18 years in hong kong, getting around home had never felt more annoying.

that's when i saw how much my mindset had changed since coming to the US. as i got used to the convenience here, i'd started taking the easy path by default. doing things the long way felt like a cost instead of a choice. i'd become impatient.

## part 2

the same pattern showed up in how i learn.

like many students, ai tools have made a lot of things easier for me. i use chat/claude to break down lectures, [clicky](https://www.heyclicky.com/) to talk me through concepts out loud, and [instinct](https://instinct.com/), an ai agent, to handle internship applications and flight bookings. none of these are bad on their own. but i started noticing that i reached for them before i'd even tried to think.

i'd put a lecture into claude, get a summary, find it too thin, then ask it to expand. i was automating my notes just to un-automate them. meanwhile the actual lecture, with the professor's tangents and the questions other students asked, was where the real teaching happened. i'd been treating the friction as waste when it was actually the point.

recently i came across the term "meat proxy": a person who exists to carry out what a machine decides. that's what my workflow had started to look like. i'd paste a question into an llm, it would spit out an answer, and i'd paste that answer somewhere else. thinking was happening, just not in my head. it described a version of myself i didn't want to become.

i see it in tech more broadly too. there's real pressure in the bay area to ship, ship, ship, and it's easier than ever to build something impressive without fully owning how it works. i notice it at hackathons, where it's common to see someone paste in a prompt like "generate the best idea that maximizes my chances of winning."

when i hosted my first hackathon in hong kong, students built things like a crosswalk reader for the visually impaired, or a fake calculator app that quietly surfaced emergency contacts for people facing domestic violence. they weren't the most polished projects, but you could tell the builders cared deeply about the problem. here, that care can get buried under the race to build the flashiest agent.

i don't think people care any less. there's so much talent in those rooms, and i keep wondering what would happen if more of it pointed at problems like the unhoused or the unfed.

looking back, the common thread was never really ai. it was speed. from the scooter to my lecture notes, i just wanted to get there faster. it even crept into how i relax. movies became shows, then youtube, then reels. sitting with nothing to do started to feel almost physically uncomfortable. even writing this took a few attempts, because staying with one thought for an hour felt foreign.

that's what getting lost in hong kong was really about. the bus routes hadn't changed. i had. i'd lost my patience for the slow parts, like waiting for a bus or not knowing the answer yet, and those were the parts where i used to learn the most.

## part 3

so i've been practicing the slow parts again, on purpose!

i walk to class a few days a week now. it takes twenty minutes instead of five, and those twenty minutes have become some of my best thinking time.

i go to lecture in person and take notes by hand. when i use ai to study, i ask it to quiz me rather than explain, so the thinking stays mine. before i reach for a summary, i read the source, whether that's the paper or the full docs.

for projects, i hold myself to one standard: if i can't walk someone through every part of it, i'm not done. it's made me a bit slower to ship and a lot better at explaining what i've built.

i'm also practicing boredom. walks without headphones, a quiet few minutes before i open my phone in the morning. it's uncomfortable at first, and that discomfort is how i know it's working.

none of this means stepping away from ai. i build with it every day, and i'm genuinely excited about where it's going. what's changed is the order. i think first, then i bring the tools in.

next time i'm home, i'm taking the bus. and i'm leaving early XD
