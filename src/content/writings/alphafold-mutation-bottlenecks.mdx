AlphaFold2 can predict the shape of nearly any protein on Earth, but it can't tell you what happens when you change one amino acid in it. Why?

In 2020, a neural network effectively solved a 50-year-old problem in biology: Give AlphaFold2 a protein sequence and
it returns a 3D structure often accurate within the width on an atom. What previously took months or years to get structures
experimentally (X-ray crystollography, cro-EM) now can be done in minutes to hours computationally. At CASP14 (2020), the blind
structure prediction competition, AlphaFold2 hit a median backbone error of under 1Å, often close to experimental accuracy. The next best method was around 2.8 Å.

When I first read about AlphaFold2, I assumed diagnosing genetic disease was basically solved:
can't we just swap in the mutation, fold the protein, see what breaks? But I was wrong - and it turns
out, the answer was hiding behind how AlphaFold works.



## How does AlphaFold work?
AlphaFold2 doesn't just fold proteins from physics. It's most important input is a
**multiple sequence alignment**: thousands of versions of the same protein from different species, lined up. When
two positions in a protein mutate together across evolution, that's a strong hint that they touch in 3D. If
one changes, the other has to compensate, or the protein falls apart.

The model's core block, the **Evoformer**, is built to mine exactly this. It keeps a running guess of which residue pairs are close,
and refines it with "triangle" updates: if residue A is near B and B is near C, then A and C can't be far apart. I found this to be the most
elegant part of the paper.

Following that, the model treats each amino acid as a small rigid piece with a position and a facing direction. 
It then slides and turns them into a chain. Its attention (Invariant Point Attention) gives the same answer no matter
how you spin the whole protein. The finished guess gets fed back in a few times to sharpen it, and a confidence score (pLDDT)
from 0 to 100 is generated.



## The catch
That evolutionary signal describes the protein *family*, not just your specific copy. Change one amino acid and the alignment
barely moves - thousands of sequences still vote for the same fold. So AlphaFold mostly predicts the same structure for the mutant and
the original, even when the mutation is know to cause disease.

Follow-up work tested this directly and found AlphaFold2's predicted structures don't reliably capture the effects of a single missense mutations.
For example, a single letter change in your CFTR gene, and you have cystic fibrosis. Ask AlphaFold2 what that change does to your protein, and it'll
hand you back a structure that looks almost exaclty like the healthy one. That's not a bug! It's a model doing exactly what it's trained for: predicting
the shape a sequence family converges on.

This approach has several bottlenecks. Although largely solving the question of structure prediction, AlphaFold has yet to solve the *how* and *why* it gets there.
Specifically, by depending on evolutionary signal, it has yet to solve the physical pathway from a floppy chain to a folded shape, and why it took that route. 
This also causes proteins with few relatives (ie. shallow MSAs) to be predicted worse.

AlphaFold also outputs one static structure, not the range of shapes a protein moves through. Proteins often go through conformational changes, with many having no
stable structure at all (disordered regions), which makes this a huge bottleneck.


## Why does this matter?
Many disease mutations don't wreck a protein's fold. They nudge stability, breaking a binding site, or shift how the protein moves. A single static
structure misses that, and the confidence score (pLDDT) measures how sure the model is about the fold, not whether the fold survives your mutation.
