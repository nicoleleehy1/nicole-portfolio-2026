1. Introduction

A popular Cantonese tradition my family follows is “Yum Cha”, which directly translates to “drinking chinese tea” while eating dim sum. In particular, we often order Pu’er tea as there is a common conception that it aids weight loss after eating fatty foods. Pu’er tea, which falls under the category of dark teas, is microbially fermented from the leaves of Camellia sinensis (“Pu’er Tea”).

2. Background information

2.1 Triglyceride Hydrolysis and the role of Pancreatic Lipase

During “Yumcha”, people eat a wide range of fatty Dim Sum. Dishes such as barbecue pork puffs (叉燒酥) and egg tarts (蛋撻) are considered some of the highest-saturated fat Dim Sums (“Nutrient Values of Chinese Dim Sum”), which contain high lipid content and are a high source of triglycerides.

Triglycerides are esters formed when the carboxyl group (-COOH) of three molecules of fatty acids are linked to the hydroxyl groups (-OH) of one glycerol via ester bonds (“Triglyceride Structure”) (Figure 1). Fatty acids digested from stored triglycerides can be metabolised in response to energy demands from cells. Triglyceride Hydrolysis is the catabolic reaction that breaks down triglyceride lipids. This involves one lipid molecule binding to the specific active site of a lipoprotein lipase (LPL) enzyme (Mead et al.). Lipase is a biological catalyst that speeds up the rate of triglyceride hydrolysis by providing an alternative pathway with lower activation energy. The enzyme adds three water molecules to break down one triglyceride ester into one glycerol and three fatty acids in the presence of acid and heat, which is optimal under biological conditions (“Triglyceride Structure”).

Triglyceride hydrolysis produces fatty acids in the body, causing more to be absorbed or stored in adipose tissue. As modern day diets contain high proportion of fats, excessive concentration of fatty acids causes high amounts of low-density lipoproteins (LDLs) to carry cholesterol from the liver to the body, and LDL particles will form deposits underneath the epithelial layer of arteries. The accumulation of fat will lead to the development of plagues which can restrict blood flow, and cause problems such as obesity.

2.2 Mechanism for Weight Loss

One major mechanism for weight loss is the inhibition of lipase (Liu, Tian, et al.), which has been proposed to reduce the breaking down of triglycerides and subsequently reduce the amount of fatty acids absorbed by the adipose tissue.

An enzyme inhibitor can be competitive or non-competitive. Competitive inhibitors can bind directly to the active site of the lipase enzyme, blocking the entry of its complementary substrate so that the substrate does not form its products. However, this process is reversible - as the concentration of triglyceride increases, more substrates can outcompete inhibitors, and hence the same Vmax, ie. the highest rate exhibited by the enzymatic reaction without an inhibitor, can be reached (Figure 2). Non-competitive inhibitors bind to the enzyme’s allosteric site, causing a change in the shape of the active site so that it is no longer specific to the substrate. In this case, triglycerides can no longer form an enzyme-substrate complex with lipase, so even an increase in substrate concentration will not change the maximum rate of reaction. Hence, Vmax is reduced and less triglycerides are broken down even at higher substrate concentrations (Figure 2).

2.3 Components of Pu’er tea and Milk

Major components of Pu’er tea include strictinin, caffeine, epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), catechins, free amino acids and soluble sugars (“Pu’er tea”) (Wang et al.). Findings have shown that polyphenol components, a class found in most dark teas, are reducing agents that act as strong antioxidants aiding “anti-obesity effects” (Aloo et al). Unlike other teas, catechins are not the abundant constituent in Pu’er tea - in fact, it was found to be a minor component, having less of an antioxidant effect than catechins in other teas. Instead, Strictinin (Figure 3) represents the major phenolic compound in Pu’er tea and research has shown that this component has the best obesity prevention effect (Tzen). Strictinin, a hydrolysable tannin from the ellagitannin family, has exhibited inhibitory potency on mouse pancreatic lipase activity (Chen, Tzu-Yin et al). Research has proposed that the mechanism for “anti-obesity effects” is the blocking of fat absorption by Strictinin inhibiting lipase in the small intestine (Tzen) both competitively and non-competitively.

Milk is chosen for this experiment to simulate the fat digested in the body. Full-fat milk contains a rich source of lipids, including 98% of lipids being triglycerides (Liu, Li, et al.). Hence, milk is chosen to provide the source of triglycerides in this experiment when measuring the rate of triglyceride hydrolysis. Theoretically, as the concentration of Pu’er tea added to milk increases, the concentration of polyphenol Strictinin will also rise, causing a larger inhibition of lipase activity and decreasing the rate of triglyceride hydrolysis. With fewer triglyceride substrates being able to bind to lipase, fewer fatty acid and glycerol products are formed. As fatty acids are the main contributor to decreasing the pH, a lower rate of triglyceride hydrolysis would cause a smaller decrease in pH. Hence, we can investigate whether Pu’er tea aids the mechanism of weight loss by varying the concentration of Pu’er tea added to milk and lipase. The rate of lipase activity can be measured using the change of pH divided by the duration of this pH change.

3. Hypothesis

Alternative Hypothesis (H1): There is a statistically significant negative relationship between the concentration of Pu’er tea and the rate of lipase-catalysed triglyceride hydrolysis at 37.0oC. As the concentration of Pu’er tea increases, the concentration of strictinin also increases, causing more competitive and non-competitive inhibition of lipase enzymesand reducing the mean rate of triglyceride hydrolysis. As a result, less fatty acids are formed after incubation at 37oC for 90 minutes. Hence, at higher concentrations of Pu’er tea, the decrease in pH will be smaller.

Null Hypothesis (H0): There is no statistically significant difference in the mean rate of lipase-catalysed triglyceride hydrolysis at 37.0oC when the reaction is conducted in varying Pu’er tea concentrations.

Controlled Variables

Controlled Variable

Method of Control

Why is control necessary?

Volume of milk

Using a 10.0 + 0.1 cm3 measuring cylinder, measure 10.0 cm3 of milk and add to a beaker. Then, use the same measuring cylinder, measure another 5.0 cm3 of milk and add to the same beaker. The total absolute uncertainty would be +0.2cm3.

Varying the volume of milk changes the concentration of triglycerides present in the solution. Increasing triglyceride concentration means more triglycerides bind to the active site and outcompete polyphenol competitive inhibitors. Hence, the rate of lipase-catalysed triglyceride hydrolysis would increase and the experiment would reduce in accuracy.

Source of milk

To ensure triglyceride substrates are not the limiting factor of enzyme activity, 100% full-fat milk is used. The same carton and brand of milk (Nestle Dairy Farm 100% Fresh Milk) were used throughout the experiment to limit inconsistencies.

Various milk brands, such as full-fat and skimmed milk, contain different fat content. This corresponds to different concentrations of triglycerides, which vary the amount of triglycerides binding to the active site.

Volume of tea

Using a 10.0 + 0.1 cm3 measuring cylinder, measure 10.0 cm3 of Pu’er tea at concentrations 0.00%, 3.00%, 6.00%, 9.00%, 12.00%, 15.00%, 18.00% into solutions. 10 + 0.1 cm3 of water will be added to the control trial at 0.00%.

A larger volume of tea increases the strictinin to lipase ratio. This causes more binding of polyphenols to either the active site or allosteric site of lipases compared to other trials with the same concentration of Pu’er tea. This reduces the accuracy of the change in pH and rate of triglyceride hydrolysis reported.

Time of infusing tea

Use a stopwatch and steep the tea for 10 minutes + 1s. Start the stopwatch when water is poured into the beaker of leaves and strain after immediately 10 minutes.

Increasing the time of infusing the tea will produce a higher concentration of Pu’er tea and increase strictinin concentration. This causes the rate of triglyceride hydrolysis to be underestimated, deviating from the value of the intended concentration and reducing the accuracy of the findings.

Type of tea

Pure ripe tea leaves from the same brand (“Tea Chateau”) were used instead of raw tea leaves throughout the experiment. It is more common to ferment ripe tea leaves than raw tea leaves for Yum Cha practices.

Various brands of teas can have different compositions of polyphenols due to differences in their fermentation process. Different polyphenol concentrations can vary the inhibitory effects on lipases, reducing the reliability of raw data.

Mass and concentration of lipase solution

Measure 1.00 + 0.01g of 5% lipase solution using a 2-decimal place electronic balance.

Varying mass and concentration of lipase causes the number of lipase per unit volume to change, causing the rate of triglyceride hydrolysis to be inaccurate.

Time of incubation

Using a stopwatch, the solutions will be incubated in the water bath for 90min + 1s. Put all boiling tubes in the same rack so that the tubes are in the bath at the same time.

Varying durations may change the number of collisions between triglycerides and lipases. Increasing time increases the likelihood of successful collisions, hence forming more glycerol and fatty acids, causing a larger change in pH.

Temperature of incubation

Solutions will be incubated in a 37.0 +0.5oC water bath to simulate the pancreatic enzymatic environment. The optimum temperature of lipase activity is also 37oC. (Kumar et al.)

Increasing temperatures will increase the kinetic energy of lipase and triglycerides. This increases collision frequency, causing more successful collisions and increasing the rate of triglycerides breaking down, deviating from the accurate value.

8. Methodology

8.1 Tea preparation:

  1. Fill a 1000cm3 kettle to the maximum line so that approximately 1000cm3 of water and boil.
  2. Remove pure Pu’er tea leaves from capsules and pour them onto a weighing boat. Using a two-decimal place (+ 0.01g) electronic balance, measure 0.00, 3.00, 6.00, 9.00, 12.00, 15.00, and 18.00g of tea leaves and transfer into each of the seven 500cm3 beakers.
  3. After the water has boiled, measure 100cm3 of boiled water using a 100 + 1cm3 measuring cylinder and pour it into each of the 500cm3 beakers.
  4. Infuse the tea for 10 minutes + 1 second, then immediately strain the tea leaves by pouring the tea through a strainer into another 500cm3 beaker. Repeat to prepare 0.00%, 3.00%, 6.00%, 9.00%, 12.00%, 15.00%, 18.00% concentrations of Pu’er tea.

8.2 pH probe preparation:

  1. Ensure that the electrode of the pH probe is not damaged.
  2. Wash the electrode using a pipette and distilled water to remove external impurities. Using dry paper towels, gently pat the pH probe to dry.
  3. Open LoggerLite on a laptop and connect the pH probe device. The program will show the pH value.
  4. Place the pH probe into a beaker of pH 4 buffer solution. Ensure that the pH reading has stabilised on the screen before calibrating the probe to accept the pH as 4.
  5. Repeat steps 2 - 4 with a beaker of pH 7 buffer solution.

8.3 Making the solution

  1. Using a 10.0 + 0.1 cm3 measuring cylinder, measure 10.0 cm3 of milk and add to a beaker. Then, use the same measuring cylinder, measure another 5.0 cm3 of milk and add to the same beaker.
  2. Transfer the milk into a 100cm3 beaker. Repeat 34 more times to prepare 35 beakers of milk.
  3. Using seven 10.0 + 0.1cm3 measuring cylinders, measure 10.0cm3 of Pu’er tea from each of the seven concentrations (0.00, 3.00, 6.00, 9.00, 12.00, 15.00, 18.00 %) and pour into 7 separate beakers of milk.
  4. Using a glass rod, stir each beaker to mix the tea and milk.
  5. Repeat steps 3-4 for 5 trials so that there are five beakers of milk with the respective 7 concentrations.
  6. Pour the milk and tea solution from the 100cm3 beaker into a boiling tube. Place the tube into the now empty 100cm3 beaker and place the setup on a 2-decimal place electronic balance. Press “tare”.
  7. Using a pipette, add 1.00 + 0.01g of 5% lipase solution into the boiling tube. Add a rubber stopper and invert the boiling tube 10 times to mix the solution of milk, tea and lipase evenly.
  8. Repeat steps 6-7 for all 35 beakers. Label each boiling tube with its trial number and tea concentration.
  9. Calibrate the pH probe and place the electrode in each boiling tube. Record the initial pH value in a table.
  10. Distribute and place all 35 boiling tubes in two test tube racks. Place both racks in the 37.0oC water bath at the same time and start the stopwatch. Incubate the boiling tubes for 90 minutes.
  11. Take the racks out of the water bath and invert each boiling tube 10 times.
  12. Calibrate the pH probe and place the electrode in each boiling tube. Record the final pH value in the table.

When steeping the tea, the darkness of the tea noticeably increases as the concentration of Pu’er tea increases. When milk and Pu’er tea were mixed, milk with more concentrated teas had a darker shade of brown (Figure 5). After 90 minutes of incubating milk with Pu’er tea and lipase at 37oC (Figure 6), yellowish precipitates formed at the bottom of each boiling tube. As milk contains proteins, the heat from the hot tea may have caused the denaturation of proteins, subsequently causing their structure to unfold and protein to aggregate. Hence, the precipitate is the result of milk proteins being suspended and clumped together (“Protein Aggregate”). Moreover, the solutions produced a rancid odour from the process of hydrolytic rancidity. This indicates the production of fatty acids from triglyceride hydrolysis.

11. Conclusion

This research investigates how changing the concentration of Pu’er tea affects the enzymatic activity of lipase-catalysed triglyceride hydrolysis by measuring the rate of pH change of milk at 37oC. The raw data table (Table 5) shows that, as concentration of Pu’er tea increases, the rate of change in pH becomes smaller. Moreover, the mean rate of lipase-catalysed triglyceride hydrolysis calculated in the Processed Data Table (Table 6) decreases as the concentration of Pu’er tea increases, aligning with the Alternative Hypothesis (H1). This is also supported by Graph 1, which shows a negative linear relationship between the concentration of Pu’er tea and the mean rate of triglyceride hydrolysis, explained with the equation of the linear regression line . A high R2 value of 0.9827 for this linear regression line also indicates that this negative relationship is strong. Further analysis with the Pearson correlation coefficient also shows that the association between both variables is strong, with the value of -0.9913 being greater than the critical value of 0.874 at significance level = 0.01.

The error bars in Graph 1 represent the standard deviations of trials in each concentration group. As Graph 1 shows, the error bars between the lowest concentration (0.00%) and highest concentration (18.00%) of Pu’er tea do not overlap, hence suggesting that the difference between concentration of Pu’er tea has a significant effect on the mean rate of triglyceride hydrolysis. However, since the error bars of the 12.00% concentration overlap with the error bars at 9.00% and 15.00%, an ANOVA test was calculated to determine whether the differences in the rate of reactions were statistically significant when varying Pu’er tea concentrations. The ANOVA summary suggests the F-ratio and p-value which can demonstrate whether the differences in data is statistically significant. A larger F-ratio means that the chance that the data gathered is less likely random and more because of the effect from manipulating the independent variable. A high F-ratio of 68.25 was calculated, which was larger than the F-critical value of 2.45, showing that there is a very high chance that not all group means are equal. The p-value (2.00 10-15) is also significantly smaller than the significance value of 0.05, which allows us to reject the null hypothesis (H0) and accept the alternative hypothesis (H1), showing that there is a statistically significant difference in rate of triglyceride hydrolysis when concentration of Pu’er tea is changed.

Strictinin was shown to be thermally labile. During Pu’er tea infusion, strictinin can completely decompose to ellagic acid and gallic acid when its temperature is above 80oC (Chen, Guan-Heng et al.) (Figure 7). As my experiment included steeping the tea with boiling water, I researched these two hydrolysis products and found out that both ellagic and gallic acid were observed to directly inhibit pancreatic lipase activity as active constituents in vitro. Research experiments have been conducted regarding the nature of inhibition caused by these two molecules. Lineweaver-Burk plots, which are typically constructed to distinguish between the two types of inhibitions, suggest that gallic acid is a competitive inhibitor (Rahim et al.) while ellagic acid acts as a non-competitive inhibitor (Wang, Shuai, et al.) to lipase.

Gallic acid was observed to contain the galloyl moiety, which has a similar chemical structure to triglyceride and hence allows for complementary enzyme-inhibitor binding to occur (Rahim et al.). The active site of lipase contains a catalytic triad, namely amino acids Ser152-His263-Asp176 (Figure 11), in which triglycerides bind to. It is suggested that gallic acid blocks the entry of triglycerides to the active site of lipase via binding to R groups that are close to the active site (at GLU-253) (Yu et al) (Figure 8). In contrast, ellagic acid is a non-competitive inhibitor of lipase, forming Hydrogen bonds and London Dispersion Forces with the allosteric site of lipase (Wang, Shuai, et al.) (Figure 9). This will initiate a conformational change in the shape of the active site of lipase, causing it to no longer become complementary to triglycerides and decreasing the efficacy of the enzyme. Triglycerides will no longer be able to bind to lipase and undergo lipase-catalyzed hydrolysis. Both the competitive inhibitory nature of gallic acid and the non-competitive inhibitory nature of ellagic acid decrease lipase activity, subsequently reducing the production of fatty acids and glycerol products. Hence, in my experiment, as the concentration of Pu’er tea increases, the concentration of gallic acid and ellagic acid also increases, subsequently explaining why the decrease in pH of milk becomes smaller.

12.2 Limitations

The structure of pancreatic lipase contains many components (Figure 10), including the lipase lid and colipase, which are also involved in triglyceride hydrolysis. The lid is responsible for protecting the active site (Khan et al.). As the active site is hydrophobic, the lid tends to be closed in aqueous medium whereas it is in open form when exposed to a hydrophobic layer (Akram et al.). Lipase lids are hence responsible for catalytic activity. Similarly, colipase is a protein cofactor involved in lipid hydrolysis. Colipase binds to the C-terminal of pancreatic lipase, stabilising the active conformational change of lipase and increasing the exposure to the overall hydrophobic binding site. The presence of the lid and presence of colipase may present opportunities for other modes of inhibition that are not accounted for in this experiment. There may be other components of Pu’er tea that affect the catalytic function of other components of lipase, and not necessarily the active site.

Moreover, as mentioned in the Background Information (2.3), Pu’er tea contains many active ingredients such as caffeine, catechins, epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) and other polyphenols. Many findings have identified that other components of Pu’er tea have an effect on the mechanism of weight loss by lipase inhibition or other means. For example, theabrownin, a water-soluble polyphenol oxidized from catechins during Pu’er fermentation (Chen, Xiaoqiang, et al.), has also be shown to be a competitive inhibition of lipase. Although the concentration of catechins in Pu’er tea is small, it may have more of an effect on lipase inhibition than strictinin. Similarly, EGCG, which has a similar concentration as strictinin in Pu’er tea (Liao et al.), was also reported as a non-competitive inhibitor to lipase (Bello et al.). These components are not accounted for in this experiment, and it would be important to isolate strictinin polyphenol extracts to draw an accurate conclusion.

Random

The technician prepared the lipase solution, so the uncertainty of concentrations used was unknown. Hence, the percentage uncertainty could have been higher if the apparatuses used were not precise.

Prepare my own solutions using lipase powder, 3 d.p. balance and 100cm3 volumetric flasks. Record the uncertainties of the apparatuses used and add to calculations.

pH probe deviation

Systematic

The pH value generated from the pH probe deviates and is often lower than the actual value after some time. As I measured the pH of trials from highest to lowest concentration, this may mean that the rate of change in pH becomes smaller because lower concentrations have a smaller measured final pH over time.

Calibrate the probe more often or use a newer pH probe. This will increase the accuracy for measuring pH.

Different temperatures of boiling water are used to prepare tea

Systematic

Teas of different concentrations are prepared at different times after the water is boiled. Since the tea is steeped for a standard time of 10 minutes, higher temperature may cause higher kinetic energy of tea leaf particles and water, hence causing the concentration to be higher.

Try to minimise the time in between preparing each tea or reboil the water again before preparing each tea concentration.

Water bath did not reach target temperature of 37oC

Systematic

The temperature of the 37oC water bath fluctuated throughout the investigation as the air conditioning was not regulated. At lower temperatures, the kinetic energy of triglycerides and lipase enzymes decreases, reducing the number of successful collisions and hence decreasing the rate of triglyceride hydrolysis. Hence, reported values of rates may be lower than actual values.

Place a thermometer and monitor the water bath throughout the experiment. Regulate the room temperature by maintaining the air conditioning at a certain temperature and use water bath lids to minimise significant heat loss.

Time lag between removing the boiling tubes from the water bath and measuring pH

Systematic

The time lag between removing the boiling tubes from the water bath and measuring the pH may have had more lipid-catalysed triglyceride hydrolysis to occur, as temperature is still around 37oC. Moreover, this may increase the duration of “incubation time”, which was not accounted.

Measure the pH of solutions in the water bath to minimise this time lag or stop the stopwatch measuring incubation time when the pH probe is placed in the solution and record the incubation time. This will produce more precise calculations.

Surface area of triglyceride fats not controlled

Systematic

Triglycerides exist in droplets of random sizes. Higher SA:V ratio of fat will result in higher rate of triglyceride hydrolysis, increasing inaccuracy.

Conduct experiment in presence of bile salts, which can emulsify fat. This allows for greater pH change and reduces percentage uncertainty.

11.4 Extension

A possible extension would be to conduct the same experiment investigating how varying concentrations of other dark teas, such as green tea, black tea and oolong tea, will affect lipase activity. Similar to Pu’er tea, these teas are best known to aid weight loss (Ajmera), but contain much higher concentrations of catechin polyphenols instead of strictinin. Upon research, tea catechins also have antioxidant properties (Higdon), and its derivative compounds (EGCG, EGC, ECG and EC) have mixed competitive and non-competitive inhibition to lipase (dos Reis), which is similar to Strictinin’s derivatives - gallic acid and ellagic acid. By comparing the rate of lipase-catalysed triglyceride hydrolysis between different teas, we can conclude which tea has the best weight loss effects for reducing obesity and promoting better health.
