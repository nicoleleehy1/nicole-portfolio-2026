Aster ranks candidate molecules with about the simplest model in cheminformatics: binary Morgan fingerprints and a linear ridge regression. I didn't start there out of conviction. I started there as a baseline, and then every attempt to replace it with a pretrained neural encoder lost. This piece goes through the comparison at a technical level, including a numerical bug that made the first result look worse than it was, and the published work that says I may still be wrong.

## The comparison

The task is low-data batch selection: start from 32 or 64 measured compounds on one target, pick 8 per round from a pool of several hundred for 5 rounds, and count how many of the pool's top-decile compounds are found. All arms in a case share the same initial set, pool, audit set and seed.

The arms:

- **Morgan + ridge.** RDKit Morgan fingerprints, radius 2, chirality-aware, folded to 2,048 binary bits. Features are standardized using measured compounds only, then fitted with ridge at alpha 10.
- **Morgan + random forest.** Same features, a tree ensemble instead of a linear model.
- **CheMeleon + ridge.** CheMeleon is a Chemprop directed message-passing network pretrained to predict Mordred molecular descriptors. I took its frozen, mean-aggregated embedding and fitted the same ridge head on top.
- **Fine-tuned CheMeleon.** In a matched pilot, the encoder and a linear head were updated for 5 CPU epochs per round on the observed labels, then the ridge head was refit on the adapted features.

## Results

| Study | Targets | Morgan + ridge | Morgan + forest | CheMeleon | Random |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Milestone 2 (24 cases) | CHEMBL1871, 2034 | **12.75** | 11.29 | 11.38 (unstable head) | 3.42 |
| Milestone 3 (24 cases) | 5-HT1A, GSK-3β, CLK4 | **18.67** | 17.33 | 15.50 (repaired head) | 4.92 |
| Milestone 3 pilot (6 cases) | same | **17.33** | 16.17 | frozen 15.33, fine-tuned 13.67 | 5.50 |

In milestone 3 the repaired CheMeleon head lost to Morgan + ridge in 15 of 24 matched cases, tied 3 and won 6. Fine-tuning tied the frozen model in four pilot cases and lost in two. It never helped.

Two caveats belong next to that table. Ridge beats the forest by only 0.25 to 1.46 compounds per 40 picks across milestones 2 to 4, while the forest had slightly *lower* audit error in milestones 3 and 4. So ridge is the discovery winner, not the accuracy winner. And the effect is target-dependent: on published GSK-3β splits CheMeleon found 14.75 against ridge's 13.50, while on 5-HT1A scaffold-transfer splits it found 9.00 against 18.50.

## The numerical failure

The first CheMeleon result had an audit MAE of **5.60** pKi units, against about 0.78 for the fingerprint models. That's not a weak model; it's a broken one.

A post-hoc diagnostic on the development task found the cause. Standardization was fitted on the 32 measured compounds only, which is correct for avoiding leakage. But one embedding dimension had a training standard deviation of about **1.4 x 10^-6**. Dividing by that turned ordinary values at prediction time into standardized features up to about **138,914**, and predictions ranged from -1,116 to 11.2 pKi. Dense learned embeddings have near-constant dimensions on small, chemically narrow sets, and z-scoring amplifies them catastrophically. Binary fingerprints rarely hit this, since a bit that's constant in training is simply zero after centering.

Milestone 3 fixed this with a pre-registered six-head sweep on the development target only. The winner removes features whose training standard deviation is below 0.01 before standardizing, recomputed each round from revealed labels only. Prediction-guard violations fell from 1,110 to zero, development audit MAE from 4.76 to 0.89, and discovery from 10.33 to 14.83. The repaired head is what the milestone 3 row uses, and it still lost. So the instability was real but wasn't the whole story: stability was necessary, not sufficient.

## Why the simple model wins in this regime

**Ridge is the right estimator for p much greater than n.** With 2,048 features and 32 to 72 labels, ordinary least squares is undefined and flexible models have plenty of room to fit noise. Ridge's solution

```text
w = (XᵀX + alpha·I)⁻¹ Xᵀy
```

is always well-posed, and it's equivalent to the posterior mean of a Bayesian linear model with a Gaussian prior on the weights, or of a Gaussian process with a linear kernel. That puts it in the same kernel-method family as the SVM-on-ECFP models that did best on MoleculeACE activity cliffs ([van Tilborg et al., 2022](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acs.jcim.2c01073)).

**Pretraining's advantage is small and fades fast.** A 2026 re-benchmark of the 30 MoleculeACE tasks put the best pretrained model (KPGT) at R² 0.678 against 0.668 for XGBoost on Morgan counts ([Zhao et al., 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13321-026-01217-2)). In OpenADMET's 2026 simulated campaigns, CheMeleon's head start over training from scratch was gone by about 200 labels ([OpenADMET](https://openadmet.ghost.io/model-take-the-wheel-active-learning-with-pec50-data/)). My replays sit at 32 to 104 labels, where pretraining should help *most*, and it still didn't. My inference is that CheMeleon's descriptor-prediction pretraining doesn't align well with these targets' structure–activity relationships, rather than that I lacked data to adapt it, but I haven't tested that directly.

**Activity cliffs favour substructure features.** MoleculeACE was built around pairs of near-identical molecules with large potency differences. A hashed substructure bit can flip on exactly the small change that matters, whereas a smooth learned embedding tends to place near-identical molecules close together, which is what you want for most properties and not for cliffs.

**Interpretability is part of the objective.** Aster's product question is "why did we test this compound?" A ridge prediction decomposes exactly into a sum of per-bit contributions, and every pick can be shown next to its nearest measured analogue by Tanimoto similarity. One caveat: folded bits collide, so a bit doesn't map to a unique substructure, and I don't yet claim fragment-level attributions.

## The case against

The strongest published challenges, and why each doesn't settle it:

| Challenger | Claim | Why it doesn't transfer directly |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [CheMeleon](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.15792) (Burns et al.) | Best or tied on 29 of 30 MoleculeACE tasks | Trained end to end on 615 to 3,657 molecules per task. I used a ridge head on 32 to 104. My negative is about my recipe at my data size |
| [TabPFN on CheMeleon features](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.16123) (2026) | Best or tied on all 30 MoleculeACE tasks; Morgan inputs clearly weaker | Untested by me. No separate result below 1,000 labels, no ridge baseline |
| [ADKF-IFT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.02708) (Chen et al., ICLR 2023) | R² 0.34 vs 0.18 for fingerprint models at 64 labels on FS-Mol | Needs meta-training on thousands of other assays; scored on prediction, not selection |
| [Chemprop D-MPNN](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.01561) (Yang et al., 2019) | Beat forest-on-Morgan on most datasets | Same paper notes fingerprints can win below about 1,000 molecules |
| Tanimoto-kernel GP ([GAUCHE](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.04450)) | Strong Bayesian optimisation baseline with count fingerprints | Natural upgrade from ridge; value depends on calibrated uncertainty, which has been weak in my data |

The pattern: most wins are on prediction accuracy rather than selection, and with more labels or more auxiliary assays than a single early campaign has. That makes them reasons to re-test, not refutations.

## What would overturn the default

I've pre-committed to three re-tests, each on targets I haven't inspected, with the gate fixed before running and ties going to ridge:

1. **Count fingerprints.** Mine are binary (`GetFingerprintAsNumPy` on a bit generator). Nearly every strong fingerprint baseline above uses counts. It's a one-line change and the fairest test of the default itself.
2. **TabPFN on CheMeleon embeddings plus RDKit descriptors.** The best-supported challenger, no fine-tuning, CPU-friendly.
3. **CheMeleon fine-tuned as its authors recommend**, end to end with their head, so the comparison can't be blamed on my ridge-head recipe.

Beyond those, I'd reopen the question if a campaign's pool moves far from its measured chemistry, if a target accumulates around 10,000 compatible measurements, or when prospective data arrive.

## Limits of this evidence

- Retrospective, on ten public targets, nine of them Ki tasks; pools of hundreds of compounds.
- Two or three seeds per case in milestones 2 to 4, and targets within a milestone share chemistry, so cases aren't independent.
- The fine-tuning pilot was six cases with one five-epoch CPU recipe.
- Held-out targets aren't guaranteed absent from CheMeleon's own pretraining data.

What I'd take from this beyond the specific models: when a sophisticated method loses, check whether it lost or broke. Here it did both, and only separating the two made the result trustworthy.
